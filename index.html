Mercury

Mercury is the smallest planet in our solar system and the closest one to the sun. Because it is so close to the sun, it orbits around it faster than any other planet. In fact, a full year on Mercury goes by in just 88 Earth days. Even though it is the closest to the sun, it is actually not the hottest planet; that title belongs to Venus. However, Mercury still gets extremely hot during the day, the temperatures reaching up to 800 degrees Fahrenheit. At the same time, because it has almost no atmosphere to trap heat, nighttime temperatures can plunge to a freezing minus 290 degrees. The surface of Mercury looks a lot like Earth's moon because it is covered in craters, cliffs, and plains. These craters were formed long ago when space rocks and comets crashed into the planet. Mercury is a rocky planet, which means it has a solid surface that you could theoretically stand on. It also has a very large iron core that takes up most of the planet's interior. Two spacecraft, named Mariner 10 and MESSENGER, have traveled to Mercury to take pictures and study it up close. Scientists are still fascinated by this little rocky world and continue to learn new secrets about how our solar system formed.

Venus

Venus is the second planet from the Sun and is often called Earth's twin because of their similar size and structure. However, Venus is a brutally hostile world with a thick, toxic atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide. Its skies are filled with dangerous clouds of sulfuric acid that trap solar heat in a runaway greenhouse effect. This makes Venus the hottest planet in the entire solar system, with surface temperatures soaring around 900 degrees Fahrenheit. The atmospheric pressure on the surface is so immense that it is equal to being nearly half a mile underwater on Earth. Venus spins in the opposite direction of most planets, meaning the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east. In addition, its rotation is so extremely slow that a single day on Venus lasts longer than its entire year. Despite the crushing environment, robotic probes like Venera and Magellan have mapped its surface, revealing thousands of active volcanoes. Scientists study Venus closely to understand how a rocky planet so similar to Earth could transform into an extreme furnace world.

Earth

Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only known world in the universe capable of supporting life. It possesses vast liquid water oceans that cover roughly 71 percent of its surface, regulating global temperatures and climate. The protective atmosphere is rich in nitrogen and oxygen, allowing plants, animals, and humans to thrive. Earth's active magnetic field acts as a global shield, protecting the surface from harmful solar radiation and cosmic rays. It features dynamic tectonic plates that continually reshape landmasses, create mountains, and trigger volcanic activity. Earth is orbited by one large natural satellite, the Moon, which stabilizes Earth's axial tilt and produces ocean tides. Equipped with moderate temperatures, liquid oceans, and a breathable atmosphere, Earth provides perfect conditions for living organisms. Humanity continues to explore space using satellites, space stations, and planetary probes while studying Earth to preserve its environment.

Mars

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and is widely known as the Red Planet due to iron oxide rust on its surface. It is a cold, dusty desert world with a very thin atmosphere composed mainly of carbon dioxide. Mars hosts extreme geographical features, including Olympus Mons, the largest volcano in the solar system, and Valles Marineris, a massive canyon. Temperatures on Mars are generally freezing, averaging around minus 80 degrees Fahrenheit across the planet. Mars is orbited by two small, oddly shaped moons named Phobos and Deimos, which may be captured asteroids. Scientific evidence shows that liquid water once flowed across Mars, forming ancient river valleys, lakebeds, and ocean basins. Multiple rovers like Curiosity and Perseverance have explored its surface to collect soil samples and search for signs of past microbial life. Space agencies around the world view Mars as the top target for future human exploration and potential colonization.

Jupiter

Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun and holds the record as the largest planet in our solar system. It is a gas giant made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium gas, lacking a solid exterior surface. Jupiter is so massive that more than 1,300 Earths could fit inside it, and its gravitational pull strongly influences nearby orbital paths. The planet is famous for the Great Red Spot, a colossal storm larger than Earth that has raged for hundreds of years. Jupiter rotates remarkably fast, completing one full turn in under 10 hours, which generates intense, sweeping magnetic fields. It is surrounded by a complex system of dozens of moons, including Ganymede, Callisto, Io, and the ocean world Europa. Spacecraft such as Galileo and Juno have braved Jupiter's intense radiation belts to measure its magnetic storm environment. Astronomers view Jupiter as a planetary shield whose strong gravity often diverts hazardous comets away from the inner planets.

Saturn

Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun and the second-largest planet in our solar system. It is best recognized for its stunning, wide ring system, which is made of billions of pieces of ice, rock, and dust particles. Like Jupiter, Saturn is a giant gas ball composed mostly of light hydrogen and helium gases. Because of its low density, Saturn is the only planet in the solar system that would float if placed in an ocean of water. Saturn experiences powerful atmospheric storms with winds reaching speeds up to 1,100 miles per hour. It commands a massive fleet of moons, including Titan, which has a thick atmosphere and liquid methane lakes, and Enceladus, which spouts water geysers. The Cassini-Huygens mission spent over 13 years orbiting Saturn, providing high-resolution imaging of its icy rings and hidden satellites. Saturn remains one of the most visually breathtaking targets for optical telescopes and amateur astronomers worldwide.

Uranus

Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun and is classified as an ice giant due to its frigid, fluid interior. Its atmosphere contains high concentrations of methane gas, which absorbs red light and gives the planet a distinct pale cyan color. The upper atmosphere features clouds of hydrogen sulfide, giving the gas layers an odor resembling rotten eggs. Uranus is unique because it rotates almost completely on its side, tilted at an extreme angle of nearly 98 degrees. This severe tilt causes long, dramatic 21-year seasons where one pole experiences constant light while the other remains in pitch darkness. It possesses faint, dark planetary rings alongside a family of 28 dynamic, icy moons named after characters created by Shakespeare and Pope. The Voyager 2 spacecraft remains the only human probe to ever fly close by Uranus, sending back historic images in 1986. Scientists hope to send future outer-space missions to explore the secrets of Uranus's tilted magnetic field and cold interior.

Neptune

Neptune is the eighth and farthest known major planet from the Sun in our solar system. Named after the Roman god of the sea, this dark ice giant shines with a deep, vivid cobalt blue color due to atmospheric methane. It is an extremely stormy world experiencing the fastest wind speeds recorded anywhere in the solar system, reaching over 1,200 miles per hour. Neptune takes an incredible 165 Earth years to complete a single journey around the Sun. Its interior consists of a super-heated, dense fluid mixture of water, ammonia, and methane slush surrounding a small, rocky core. Neptune is orbited by 16 known moons, with Triton being the largest and notable for its retrograde orbit and active nitrogen gas geysers. Voyager 2 flew past Neptune in 1989, capturing photos of giant dark storms like the Great Dark Spot before heading toward interstellar space. Because it is located so far away, Neptune remains one of the least explored and most mysterious planets in the outer system.

Sun

The Sun is the massive star situated at the very center of our solar system, providing heat and energy to all surrounding worlds. It contains over 99.8 percent of all the total mass in the solar system, holding every planet, dwarf planet, and asteroid in stable orbits. The Sun produces energy through nuclear fusion in its core, converting huge amounts of hydrogen into helium every second. Core temperatures reach a blistering 27 million degrees Fahrenheit, generating pressure that releases light and energy outward. Its visible surface, called the photosphere, frequently displays dark, cooler spots known as sunspots, caused by turbulent magnetic activity. The Sun drives Earth's seasons, ocean currents, weather patterns, climate cycles, and photosynthesis necessary for life. Space missions such as the Parker Solar Probe regularly monitor solar wind streams and energetic solar flares. Without the light and gravitational stability generated by the Sun, our solar system would be a freezing, lifeless expanse.
